Recovering the timing and size of hidden cortisol secretory pulses, and the rates at which cortisol enters and leaves the blood, from noisy 24-hour serum cortisol measurements — the physiology behind it, a two-compartment model solved with coordinate descent and compressed sensing, the results across ten subjects, a validation on simulated data, and the problems faced along the way.

Why deconvolve cortisol?

Cortisol secretion is regulated by the Hypothalamus, Anterior Pituitary Gland and Adrenal Glands. The central signals from the body's circadian clock, physiological stress or the metabolic demands trigger the release of Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH) into the blood system. The CRH binds on the membrane receptors in the Anterior Pituitary Gland which results in the release of Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH). The ACTH travels through the bloodstream and binds on cells in the Adrenal Glands triggering enzymatic pathways that sequentially convert cholesterol to produce cortisol. Cortisol is a steroid hormone and can't be stored, thus it diffuses immediately out of the adrenal glands into systemic circulation after synthesis.

Cortisol enters the bloodstream in 15 to 22 discrete pulsatile bursts over a 24 hours period based on the Circadian and Ultradian Rhythms, the circadian rhythm defines the amplitude of each pulse throughout the day, the pulses are largest near awakening and reduce throughout afternoon and evening, reaching the least around midnight while the ultradian rhythm depicts the frequency of these secretory burst which occur at intervals of around 60 to 90 minutes.

Cortisol regulates it's own secretion through two negative feedback mechanisms, firstly, Fast Feedback, within minutes of pulse, cortisol acts directly on membrane receptors of hypothalamus and pituitary to prevent the release of prepared CRH and ACTH and secondly, delayed feedback, over hours, cortisol binds to intracellular receptors in the brain and pituitary that suppress the expression of CRH, reducing the future production of ACTH.

In circulation, cortisol undergoes transport and metabolic degradation and is inactivated in the liver.

By deconvoluting the blood cortisol levels directly quantifies the frequency (ultradian) and amplitude (circadian) patterns of the adrenal cortisol secretion, which acts as a function of ACTH and CRH pulses. The direct measurement of CRH in humans is virtually impossible because of the microscopic blood vessel connections between the hypothalamus and pituitary, while the ACTH degrades rapidly in the blood stream. By applying mathematical deconvolution to the cortisol measurements, the sparse input vector u(t) reconstructs the timing and size of the cortisol bursts triggered by the ACTH pulses in a non-invasive manner.

The data

The dataset contains the simulated noisy cortisol time-series data for 10 subjects over a 24-hour period, sampled every 10 minutes. The analysis of the data has to account for:

  1. Missing data points
  2. Potential outliers
  3. Additive noise in the data
  4. Unknown initial condition
  5. Sparsity tuning

Pre-processing the data

On observing the data, it consisted of missing values (NaN), negative values, outlier values and few subjects with high-scale values. Thus, utilizing four pre-processing steps, sequentially, unit conversion, outlier removal, negative value removal and gap filling.

Unit conversion

On observation the subjects 7, 8 and 9 have much larger values, about 28 times those of the other subjects, thereby indicating different measurement units. Cortisol has a molar mass of 362.46 g/mol, converting it to the standard unit, 1 µg/dL = 27.59 nmol/L, dividing these 3 subjects by 27.59, makes all the subjects on a single scale.

Outliers

A secretory increases several samples in 15-22 events and has a clearance half-life of about 60 – 90 minutes, so keeping the range of 45 minutes to 120 minutes, so the blood cortisol can reduce by at most about 14% in 10 minutes. Thus, a sample that rises much above the neighbouring samples and vanishes by the next sample is not physiological. After identifying the outliers, they are set to missing values.

Negative values

A sample concentration cannot be negative, so the 14 negative samples are set to missing values.

Missing values

All the missing values, including the removed outliers and negative values, are filled with shape-preserving piece-wise cubic interpolation (pchip). Unlike spline, pchip does not overshoot, spline gave a negative value for subject 1.

preprocess_signal.m

function [y145, info] = preprocess_signal(x, n, cfg)

info.subject = n;
info.converted = ismember(n, cfg.nmol_subjects);
if info.converted
    x = x / cfg.nmol_per_ugdl;
end
info.n_missing_raw = nnz(isnan(x));

prev = [NaN; x(1:end-1)];
next = [x(2:end); NaN];
higher_neighbour = max(prev, next);
step = 1.4826 * median(abs(diff(x)), 'omitnan');
info.outliers = find((x - higher_neighbour) > cfg.spike_k * step);
info.outlier_values = x(info.outliers);
x(info.outliers) = NaN;

info.sigma_hat = 1.4826 * median(abs(diff(x, 2)), 'omitnan') / sqrt(6);

info.negatives = find(x < 0);
info.negative_values = x(info.negatives);
info.n_negative = numel(info.negatives);
x(info.negatives) = NaN;

info.filled = find(isnan(x));
info.first_unavailable = isnan(x(1));
y145 = fillmissing(x, 'pchip');
end

The model

The model is a two-compartment physiological system driven by a sparse, pulsatile input vector, designed to solve a non-convex optimization problem via coordinate descent and compressed sensing, where compartment 1 is the adrenal glands that captures the rapid synthesis and secretion of cortisol triggered by ACTH stimulation, based on the de novo synthesis the initial condition for the adrenal glands is set to zero and the compartment 2 is the blood circulation which captures the circulating cortisol in blood, which contains the infused cortisol from the adrenal glands and clearance by the liver.

dx1(t)/dt = −θ1x1(t) + u(t)     Adrenal Compartment
dx2(t)/dt = θ1x1(t) − θ2x2(t)     Blood Circulation Compartment
  • x1(t) → cortisol concentration in adrenal glands
  • x2(t) → cortisol concentration in blood
  • θ1 > 0 → infusion rate adrenal gland to circulation
  • θ2 > 0 → clearance rate circulation → liver
  • Parameter constraint → 4θ2 ≤ θ1, infusion rate is at least 4 times clearance rate

Pulsatile input signal

u(t) = ∑i=1N qi δ(t − τi)
  • qi > 0 → amplitude of pulse at τi
  • δ(·) → dirac delta function
  • N = 1440 min in 24 hr day
  • Sparsity constraint → model constraints elements of u to be between 15 – 22

Model matrix

Blood sampled every 10 minutes (M = 144 observations over 24 hours, samples 2 – 145).

y = Aθy0 + Bθu + v
  • y = [y10, y20, … y10M]T → M × 1 vector of blood cortisol levels
  • y0 → initial blood cortisol level (t = 0)
  • Aθ → M × 1 vector baseline decay atk = e−θ2kΔt
  • Bθ → M × N Transfer matrix – discrete impulse response functions
  • v → M × 1 vector zero-mean gaussian noise

ss_AB_matrix.m

function [A_theta, B_theta] = ss_AB_matrix(theta, params)

Ny = 144;
Tsy = 10;
dim = 2;

Initial condition

With reference to Faghih et al. (2014), x1(0) = 0, cortisol is synthesized de novo and not stored in adrenal glands, and x2(0) = y0, the first measurement. When the first measurement is unavailable, x2(0) is estimated by non-negative least squares from the data.

estimate_y0.m

function y0 = estimate_y0(theta, u, y, params)

[A, B] = ss_AB_matrix(theta, params);
r = y(:) - B*u(:);
y0 = max(0, (A' * r) / (A' * A));
end

Estimation method

Both rate parameters θ = [θ1, θ2]T and the input pulse vector u are unknown, recovering them from observation y, is non-convex optimization problem.

minu ≥ 0, Cθ ≤ b   Jλ(θ, u) = ½‖y − Aθy0 − Bθu‖22 + λ‖u‖pp

p = 0.5 → approximate non-zero pulses.

coordinate_descent.m

function theta = Parameter_Estimation(theta, u, y,y0,lam,params)
lb = params.lb; ub = params.ub;
lb = lb(:); ub = ub(:);
fun = @(x)cost_function_interior_point(x, u, y, y0, lam, params);
x0 = theta(:);
A = [-1;4]'; b = 0;  Aeq = [];  beq = [];
nonlcon = [];
options = optimoptions('fmincon','Algorithm','interior-point','Display','off');
theta = fmincon(fun,x0,A,b,Aeq,beq,lb,ub,nonlcon,options);
end

function J = cost_function_interior_point(theta,u,y,y0,lam,params)
theta = theta(:)';
[A_theta,B_theta] = ss_AB_matrix(theta,params);
y_model = A_theta*y0 + B_theta*u;
J = 0.5*norm(y-y_model,2)^ 2+ lam*norm(u,0.5)^0.5;
end

Stage 1: Coarse initialization using FOCUSS+

Search parameter space from random initial conditions and achieve starting values of [θ0, u] for both rate parameters θ = [θ1, θ2]T and input pulse.

Random Initialization: random variable ω ∈ [0, 1] is sampled, with θ0 = [ω, ω/4]T → 4θ2 < θ1.

Pulse Vector FOCUSS+: consider θj−1 fixed, solve for uj using FOCUSS+. Uses re-weighted norm minimization to approximate L-norm (p = 0.5). Uses update regularization scheme → increase λ towards λmax as error decreases.

Sparsity Tuning: If number of non zero elements exceeds n = 22 after more than half iteration, explicitly keeps only n largest elements, truncates rest to zero.

Parameter update: with uj fixed, it solves θj = [θ1, θ2]T using interior point method.

Short Coordinate Descent: Alternate for fixed θj and uj for 30 iterations to settle rapidly for local minima without large computation.

coordinate_descent.m

w = 0.8*rand;
theta_j_1 = [w;w/4];
theta_init = theta_j_1;
y0_j = y0;
ConvergenceFlag =0;
maxiter = params.max_iter;
while (ConvergenceFlag==0) && ~pass_done
    uj_1 = ones(params.Nu,1);
    for j=1:30
        [uj, labmda] = FOCUSS_Plus(uj_1, y, y0,theta_j_1,params);
        theta_j = Parameter_Estimation(theta_j_1,uj,y,y0,labmda,params);
        if params.fit_y0, y0 = estimate_y0(theta_j, uj, y, params); end
        theta_j_1 = theta_j;
    end

FOCUSS_Plus.m

    lambda_FOCUSS = (1-min(epsilon,1)) * lambdamax;
    P = diag(abs(u).^(2-p));
    pinvPi_err = lambda_FOCUSS * eye(length(y)) + 1e-10 * eye(length(y));
    u = P * B' * ((B * P * B' + pinvPi_err) \ y_theta);

    if(index_xiter > xiter/2)
        if(num > max_Nu)
            u(u(:)<amp_threshold)=0;

Stage 2: Optimization with GCV-FOCUSS+

Achieve convergence with Generalized Cross-Validation to balance filtering against pulse sparsity.

From stage 1, candidate pair (θj, uj) that minimizes the objective functions cost Jλ(θ, u) = ½‖y − Ay0 − Bu‖22 + λ‖u‖pp is selected as initial condition (θ0, u0) for stage 2.

Adaptive Pulse Recovery via GCV-FOCUSS+: with θi−1 fixed, the pulse vector ui is updated using GCV-FOCUSS+, initialized ui−1.

Dynamic λ optimization: At each inner iteration γ, regularization parameter λ(γ+1) is re-calculated by minimizing the Generalized-Cross Validation function G(λ) over [0, 10] using Golden Search.

G(λ) = L‖(I − Hλ)yθ‖22 / (trace(I − Hλ))2
Hλ = BθPuBθT(BθPuBθT + λI)−1

Error Minimization: Minimizing G(λ) ensures that λ adapts dynamically to noise of specific subject.

Parameter Refinement: with ui fixed, θi is updated using interior point, initialized at θi−1.

Convergence: alternate in coordinate descent loop until convergence reached.

GCV_FOCUSS_Plus.m

    [R,Sigma,~] = svd(B*P^(0.5));
    zeta = R'*y_theta;
    Sing_values = diag(Sigma);
    L = length(zeta);
    lambda = fminbnd(@(lambda) G_lambda(lambda,zeta,Sing_values,L), 0, l_max);

GCV_FOCUSS_Plus.m

    function GLamb = G_lambda(lambda,zeta,sigma,L)
        s_num = 0;
        s_denum = 0;
        for i = 1:L
            s_num = s_num + (zeta(i)^2)*(lambda/(sigma(i)^2+lambda))^2;
            s_denum = s_denum + lambda/(sigma(i)^2+lambda);
        end
        GLamb = L*s_num/(s_denum^2);
    end

Selection process of the starts

As the cost function is non-convex, running the algorithm from a single starting point can result in a suboptimal local minima, leading to incorrect pulses or poor clearance rates. Thus, the two-stage process is repeated independently across 10 random initializations per subject. With the convergence of all 10 independent runs, the objective cost function is calculated for each converging candidate, ensuring the best statistical goodness fit.

deconvolve_subject.m

S = cell(cfg.n_init, 1);
log_dir = cfg.progress_dir;
parfor i = 1:cfg.n_init
    p = params;
    p.log_file = fullfile(log_dir, sprintf('S%02d_start%02d.txt', n, i));
    S{i} = coordinate_descent(y, y0, p, i, false);
end
S = [S{:}];

npulse = arrayfun(@(s) numerosity(s.u, 0), S);
valid = find(npulse >= cfg.min_pulses);
res.no_valid_start = isempty(valid);
if res.no_valid_start
    warning('Subject %d: no start with >= %d pulses; using the lowest-cost start.', n, cfg.min_pulses);
    valid = 1:cfg.n_init;
end
[~, k] = min([S(valid).cost]);
best = S(valid(k));

Validation

Validation was achieved by considering the estimates (θ, pulses, x1(0) = 0, x2(0)) as the ground truth, followed by a 24-hours simulation with the same model, sampled with every 10 minutes and a zero-mean gaussian noise was added. Then the simulated samples were pre-processed and deconvolved with the same code and settings. Finally, the recovered θ and pulses were compared with the truth.

Problems faced

Choosing the sparsity

It is observed that the noise varies distinctly across different subject in the measurement data, so in order to tune the sparsity we look to identify λmax per subject by identifying the fit error that was closest to the standard deviation of the measurement noise, but it wasn't conclusive as few subjects never reached closer to the noise and in the few subject the closest fit differed by a minimum amount resulting in an ambiguous λmax choice.

Thus, a single λmax with GCV adaptation was utilized using the L-Curve that maps the fit-error + noise against the pulse size, a method to balance the competing objective.

λmax = 0.3 gave fits closest to the noise level across subjects, with 15–22 pulses per day. We choose a range of λmax values {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10} to identify the optimal state with minimum error and limited noise level.

However, the L-Curve isn't formed properly, one because of the erratic noise behaviour and second because of the limited λmax, making it not the best way to identify the best λmax value for the subjects.

Choosing the initial condition

Initial condition is important in identifying the input pulse vector, thus estimating x1(0) and x2(0) is ideal, a non-negative least squares approach was attempted in estimating the initial conditions of both the compartments but achieve correct results when the cortisol is present in the adrenal glands but failed when a real pulse is observed in the initial measurements. Thus, we utilized the initial conditions as x1(0) = 0 with x2(0) = first measurement y0 based on the de novo synthesis as Faghih et al. (2014).

Conclusion

A two compartment model was utilized with sparse deconvolution to estimate θ1, θ2 and 14–22 secretory pulses over 24 hours for all 10 subjects with one code and one configuration. The data was pre-processed to handle units, outliers, negative values and missing samples. With the additional goal of identifying initial condition and sparsity tuning that recover signals to learn model parameters. On running deconvolution and estimating the infusion rate, clearance rate and secretory pulses, the parameters were validated on the simulated data to recover the parameters with median errors of 7.6% and 6.1% and 169 of 182 of the pulses.
